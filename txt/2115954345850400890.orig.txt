How do capability improvements transform the economy, and when? In this essay, we define thresholds of economic capabilities, e.g. minimal AGI, as a function of how they transform the economy. We then track how the economy changes during the transition across capability regimes.

The last few years have seen exponential growth in the capabilities of machine intelligence. Every few months, we see a frontier AI model clear another milestone that seemed years away. But the broader economy has unchanged. Most people still go to recognizable jobs, and aggregate productivity numbers have barely budged. Why? This remains weird to me and to many other people. AI is extremely good at a lot of things, and yet it has barely moved the performance of the economy. Things have not really changed very much.

Part of the reason for this disconnect is that the individual AI agent is the wrong economic object. The vast majority of capability benchmarks measure individual AI agents solving problems in isolation, but economic transformation is an equilibrium outcome where orgs compete, subject to the forces of supply and demand. Workers do not operate in a vacuum. Orgs assemble people with complementary strengths and divide up tasks to create teams far more capable than any solo worker. Organizations are a form of superintelligence.

One major unlock for this is agent swarms, or agent fleets if you will, which will allow AIs to more effectively work together.

At the same time, standard econ models would miss the transformational potential of AI because they still tend to model machine intelligence as previous tech innovations—either as a better tool in a worker's hands or a machine that is swapped in for people one task at a time. This is wrong for two main reasons. First, AI agents can be scaled on demand at the cost of compute. Second, the constraints that limit the size of human teams—such as span of control, communication frictions, and misaligned incentives—need not apply to machine agents.

So when does machine intelligence actually lead to economic transformation, and what capabilities need to be achieved in order to see the impact of AI on market structure, wages, and jobs? To answer this question, we derive economic scenarios under different levels of capabilities.

There are two key capability thresholds. Competence: gains in individual skills like math, writing, or coding will smoothly shift wages, prices, and the mix of humans and agents in a firm while leaving industry structure largely intact. And coordination: gains in the ability of agents to work together within a firm alter how orgs scale. Once coordination reaches a critical threshold, it triggers large, abrupt jumps in market concentration, employment, and wages.

Human capability is uneven too, but while human intelligence is jagged in different directions, AI models are jagged in ways that are very similar to one another. In a competitive market, the right question is whether an org of AI agents can surpass an org of humans, or a blend of humans and agents.

Imagine a market where firms assemble any workforce they like, hiring scarce humans at market wages and renting AI copies at the price of compute. So basically a market where firms have a centaur workforce of both humans and AIs. This is already basically how AI labs and big tech companies work.

Any org's overall capability can be decomposed into the competence and coordination dimensions discussed earlier. Competence sets the level of output per worker. Coordination determines what happens to that output as the org adds more humans or AI agents. Competence sets the potential productivity level; coordination determines how big an org can get without becoming too messy to function.

Track output per worker as an organization grows and you get what we term organizational scaling laws. So what happens when you scale a team of agents? Below a basic floor of coordination, agents cooperate so poorly with one another that solo work wins. I think we're above that level now. Now we have a bounded scale: dividing work raises output per person at smaller firms, then levels off and starts declining as the firm grows and there are too many workers for effective coordination. Increasing competence shifts the level of the curve up without changing firm size, but better coordination is what allows firms to get bigger. Adding specialists with complementary skills changes the level of productivity but not the optimal firm size; it is the addition of the coordinator that allows a bigger firm to form in equilibrium. And then if a firm gets really good at coordinating, each new member becomes more productive than the previous one. Bigger becomes better, and firm size is only constrained by the size of the market.
