# AI for scientific discovery: How researchers use Claude

_Johannes Hermle*, Ben Jones*, Lukas Althoff*, Ryan Heller, Thomas Lyttelton, Carolyn Stein, Nathan Wilmers, Maxim Massenkoff, Peter McCrory_

_*Lead authors of the report_

## Summary

*   **What we measure.** AI could speed up scientific discovery, but by how much depends on what AI can do and how scientists use it. Using privacy-preserving methods, we study 207,000 Claude and Claude Code research sessions to understand how Claude is used across scientific fields and research tasks. We are conducting this research to help the public better understand the state of AI for science today and assess the impact that AI may have on scientific discovery in the future.
*   **Where scientists use Claude.** Relative to the number of researchers in each field, Claude is used more in the formal sciences, such as computer science, mathematics, and statistics, and less in health and lab-based physical sciences. Scientists primarily use Claude to analyze data and to write. Data analysis alone accounts for about one-third of sessions on Claude and nearly half of sessions on Claude Code. Scientists use it less for idea generation and data gathering, even though, in an accompanying survey, they rank those tasks as the most important for scientific discovery. That said, specialized AI tools designed for scientific work could narrow the gap between the research tasks scientists use AI for and the ones they consider most important. On Claude Science, for instance, idea generation makes up about twice the share of use that it does on Claude.
*   **How Claude use tracks AI capability.** We create a measure of how well AI can, in theory, perform each research task. The tasks AI is best suited to (the top 20% on this measure) get two to four times as much Claude use as the time researchers spend on them would predict. The tasks AI is least suited to (the bottom 20%), which include most physical work, get very little. We observe the same pattern across fields: fields where AI can perform many tasks well, such as computer science and statistics, use Claude more.
*   **What this could mean for the speed of scientific discovery.** Using a simple model, we explore scenarios for how much AI could speed up research. If existing AI capabilities are widely adopted, they could double research output in some fields. If AI could carry out all non-physical research work, output in fields such as computer science and statistics could rise as much as 15-fold, while output in bench sciences such as chemistry and biology, which are held back by physical bottlenecks, would rise three- or fourfold. In our survey of researchers who actively use AI, about 40% expect AI models to carry out full research projects on their own by 2030.

## Introduction

Science has been described as an “endless frontier” (Bush 1945). But how fast we explore that frontier has historically been limited by a scarce resource: the time and talent of human researchers. Artificial intelligence could ease that limit by amplifying what researchers can do, transforming both the process of discovery and the pace of exploration (Wang et al. 2023; Amodei 2024). Few applications of AI could matter more for human welfare. Given the highly cognitive nature of research compared with most sectors of the economy, AI’s effects may be particularly large and early in this domain.

Recent examples show AI playing a growing role in scientific discovery. In structural biology, AlphaFold predicted the three-dimensional shapes of over 200 million proteins (Jumper et al. 2021; Varadi et al. 2024), and its creators shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In materials science, GNoME predicted 380,000 stable new crystals (Merchant et al. 2023). In mathematics, researchers have used AI systems to help resolve decades-old Erdős problems, produce a potential proof for a Millennium Prize problem, and make progress on other major problems, from open conjectures to verifying landmark proofs (Anthropic 2026a, 2026b; OpenAI 2026a, 2026b; Tsoukalas et al. 2026).

Beyond these developments, most scientific progress involves many specialized, interlinked research tasks, which can vary substantially across fields. How much AI accelerates scientific discovery depends not only on AI’s capabilities, but also on how it is adopted across these tasks. In this report, we use privacy-preserving infrastructure to study the scientific use of Claude, Claude Code, and Claude Science. We document how usage varies across fields and tasks, identify what explains these usage patterns, and outline scenarios for how AI could accelerate research.

Claude use in science varies by field. Relative to their number of researchers, formal sciences such as computer science, mathematics, and statistics use Claude more heavily, while health and lab-based physical sciences use it less. Across research tasks, scientists use Claude more for analyzing data and writing, and less for gathering data or generating ideas and hypotheses. This is consistent with self-reported AI use from an accompanying survey of scientists.

To understand these differences, we develop a measure of theoretical AI capability. Claude use is higher in tasks and fields that score higher on this measure. These also tend to be more cognitive in nature and better suited to reinforcement learning, in which AI learns from feedback on repeated attempts. In short, scientists appear to use AI where it works best.

Based on these findings, we outline scenarios for how AI could accelerate scientific progress. With current capabilities, research output in some fields could as much as double, primarily from task-level augmentation. Researchers in our survey expect much greater capabilities to come. In a hypothetical scenario where AI fully automates all non-physical work, highly cognitive fields such as computer science or statistics could see up to a 15-fold increase in output. Because the fields with the largest potential for acceleration include those that produce AI itself, these gains could create an additional feedback loop, in which faster AI research produces more capable AI, which in turn could further accelerate research.

While faster scientific discovery could bring substantial benefits to humanity, AI-driven research also carries risks and uncertainties. Less human oversight could lead to the pursuit of dangerous or unethical research. AI could also reshape researchers’ careers, making research work more or less rewarding, and alter the sense of purpose associated with the human pursuit of knowledge.

## How we conducted this research

Our goal with this research is to measure the different ways scientists use Claude while protecting the details of their work. To do this, we use privacy-preserving infrastructure to analyze the scientific use of Claude on consumer plans. A language model, used here as a “classifier,” reads each session transcript and labels it with a fixed set of categories, such as the field and the research task it involves, according to a predefined taxonomy (SciNet taxonomy by Alamos and Althoff 2026). The resulting labels, such as “chemistry” or “data cleaning,” are the only information from these conversations retained and analyzed by the research team for this report. No classifier employed in this study extracts and stores open-ended information or details on research content, such as research datasets, hypotheses, experimental methods, early findings, or unpublished papers. Results are reported only in aggregate and only for cells that meet a minimum number of distinct accounts. All classifiers introduced in this study are listed in the [Appendix](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf).

Our measurement involves two steps. A first classifier identifies whether a session transcript involves scientific work. A second classifier then assigns each of these transcripts to a field, subfield, and task in the SciNet taxonomy. SciNet contains 7,262 tasks, spanning 34 fields and 318 subfields. Some tasks in the taxonomy, such as drafting manuscripts, are universal to all fields. Others are specific to individual fields or subfields. For instance, in the taxonomy, the task of predicting optical absorption and emission spectra is specific to materials science. Together, the tasks cover the full process of producing a new research finding.

Our main sample consists of 207,049 sessions on consumer plans from August 2 to September 30, 2026, which were assigned a scientific field and a research task: 128,914 from Chat and Cowork, two general-purpose interfaces for conversation and knowledge work, hereafter referred to as “Claude,” and 78,135 from Claude Code, an agentic coding tool that works directly with a user’s files, writing and running code. We also study a supplemental sample of Claude Science sessions. For details on sample construction, see the [Appendix](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf).

Combining usage data and a structured taxonomy of research tasks allows us to study which fields adopt Claude and for which parts of the research process. This research contributes to a growing body of work attempting to measure the adoption of AI technology for scientific discovery (Kobak et al. 2025; Liang et al. 2025; Hao et al. 2026; Codreanu et al. 2026).

## How Claude use varies across scientific fields

We begin by describing how Claude use varies across scientific fields. Figure 1 compares a field’s share of sessions against the share of global researchers in that field, giving us a sense of relative use per scientific field.[1](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-researchers-use-claude#footnote-1) On Claude, the three largest fields by session share are computer science (11.9%), engineering (8.4%), and medicine (7.7%), which are also among the largest fields by global researcher counts.[2](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-researchers-use-claude#footnote-2) Overall, fields with a larger share of researchers also tend to account for a larger share of Claude sessions (log-log correlation of 0.79).

However, Claude use per field cannot be explained by the number of researchers alone. In Figure 1, fields above the 45-degree line show higher usage relative to their size, while fields below the line show lower usage. Relative usage is especially high for the formal sciences, including computer science, mathematics, and statistics. Usage is also high for the social sciences. By contrast, physical sciences such as chemistry, where work often happens in a lab, tend to show lower relative usage. So does medicine, the third-largest field by session volume, where researchers spend much of their time with patients.

On Claude Code, usage is less strongly related to field size (log-log correlation with researcher counts of 0.62) and concentrates in more quantitative fields. Despite making up only 4.3% of the world’s researchers, computer science alone accounts for 32.7% of Claude Code sessions.[3](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-researchers-use-claude#footnote-3) Other formal sciences, such as mathematics and statistics, also show high relative usage. Relative usage is low in the arts and humanities and in the social sciences, with the exception of economics, a comparatively quantitative field.

Figure 1. Claude use across scientific fields. Each dot represents a field. The horizontal axis shows the field’s share of researchers, and the vertical axis shows the field’s share of research sessions, both on log scales. The top panel shows sessions from Claude; the bottom panel shows sessions from Claude Code.

We observe similar patterns when comparing usage across 318 subfields within those 34 fields (Figure 2).[4](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-researchers-use-claude#footnote-4) This more granular subfield view reveals that artificial intelligence and machine learning stands out, with session shares of 5.0% for Claude and 15.2% for Claude Code, despite making up only 1.2% of the world’s researchers. More generally, regardless of the broad scientific domain, computational subfields are among the strongest adopters. These subfields include computational biology, computational linguistics, and computational neuroscience. Robotics also shows high relative usage, likely because it builds heavily on computational methods despite its focus on physical systems.

The horizontal axis shows the subfield’s share of researchers, and the vertical axis shows the subfield’s share of research sessions, both on log scales. ## How Claude use varies across research tasks

Beyond understanding which fields use Claude, we also want to understand what scientists use it for. Research involves many different tasks, from generating hypotheses and analyzing data to writing up findings in a research paper. To better discern how Claude supports scientific discovery, we study how usage varies across these tasks. To do so, we assign each session to one of the 7,262 tasks in SciNet. These tasks can be universal to all fields, such as drafting manuscripts, or specific to fields or subfields, such as performing differential gene expression analysis to compare tumor subtypes, which is associated with cancer biology. The taxonomy distinguishes between research tasks, which researchers perform to produce new scientific findings, and non-research tasks, such as teaching, peer review, and service to the department or field.[5](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-researchers-use-claude#footnote-5) We limit our analysis to research tasks spanning eight categories: (1) data analysis, (2) writing and communication, (3) data gathering, (4) reading and knowledge acquisition, (5) theoretical analysis, (6) design and development (of software, materials, and creative works), (7) ideation and hypothesis generation, and (8) other, such as administering research.

For each task category, we calculate its share of Claude and Claude Code sessions separately. To assess whether usage in a category is high or low, we consider the share of their time that researchers typically spend on that category, as estimated by SciNet. These time estimates are based on an LLM classifier, validated using both wet-lab research protocols that record researcher time spent per task and the frequency of tasks across thousands of academic papers (see the [Appendix](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf)). This provides a benchmark to assess whether the share of Claude use across different task categories follows or deviates from how researchers spend their time.

**Usage by task category.** Data analysis tasks have the highest usage in absolute terms and are overrepresented relative to researcher time. Data analysis accounts for 33.7% of sessions on Claude and 48.1% on Claude Code, compared with 27.6% of researcher time. Writing and communication tasks also show high usage, especially on Claude (32.0%, compared with 16.0% of researcher time).[6](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-researchers-use-claude#footnote-6) Data gathering tasks have low relative usage, accounting for 28.6% of researcher time but only 11.5% of sessions on Claude and 11.9% on Claude Code. The underrepresentation of data gathering is particularly strong for tasks with physical barriers, such as the collection of clinical or biochemical data. Data gathering tasks that can rely on more digital processes, such as retrieving archival documents or text, are less underrepresented, while purely digital data collection tasks, such as collecting online data, can be overrepresented. Ideation and hypothesis generation tasks also have low usage, accounting for 4.2% of sessions on Claude and 2.1% on Claude Code, compared with 7.1% of researcher time.

Figure 3. Claude use by scientific task category. Each row represents one of the eight task categories. Orange bars represent the share of Claude research sessions dedicated to each category; blue bars represent the same for Claude Code research sessions. The black lines represent the estimated share of research time calculated from SciNet (see the Appendix ).

**Comparing observed usage to survey evidence.** Along with our analysis of Claude and Claude Code sessions, we conducted a survey of 528 scientists, whose self-reported AI usage lines up with the patterns we observed in those sessions. The sample consists of researchers primarily in the life and health sciences who are active adopters of AI.[7](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-researchers-use-claude#footnote-7) Consistent with the Claude usage patterns shown in Figure 3, survey participants’ self-reported AI usage is highest for data analysis and writing and communication, and lower for ideation and hypothesis generation and data gathering. Participants also report more shared or delegated use of AI for data analysis and writing and communication (Figure 4).

The survey also asked which categories matter most for producing important research results. Respondents rank ideation and hypothesis generation first and data gathering second. Notably, these categories are underrepresented in self-reported usage as well as in Claude sessions. The reasons for this underrepresentation likely differ. Much of data gathering is physical, while ideation is not. For ideation, scientists may prefer generating ideas themselves, or they may perceive AI’s capabilities as limited. Our data may also undercount ideation, since it tends to be woven into many other research tasks and is difficult to classify. But AI may also be genuinely weaker at high-level ideation, such as choosing which research question to pursue and designing a study to answer it.

Figure 4. Researchers’ reported use of AI. The left panel shows the percentage of survey respondents at each level of AI use, from none to autonomous, for four research task categories. The right panel shows how the respondents rate the importance of each category for producing an important research result (allocating 100 points across the four categories).

**Biological and biomedical use of Claude Science.** While Claude and Claude Code are general-purpose interfaces, specialized AI tools for the sciences are increasingly available. To study whether such tools support more specialized and demanding research, we use our privacy-preserving analysis infrastructure to compare biology and biomedical work on Claude Science with that on Claude and Claude Code.

Claude Science connects Claude to many research tools and databases that are otherwise fragmented and acts to integrate and accelerate scientific workflows. Scientists must often use different platforms, each with unique data structures or query languages, to combine distinct tools and databases in their research. For example, biologists studying whether existing drugs can act on a specific protein must typically consult separate databases regarding the protein’s function, its structure, compounds already tested against it, and related drug trials or papers. Claude Science combines these into one workflow.

Use of Claude Science is concentrated in the life sciences. The top two fields are biomedical sciences and biology, which together comprise over 50% of sessions. Within these fields, we assess which tasks are most overrepresented relative to tasks performed by Claude.

We find that researchers use Claude Science for more specialized tasks than Claude (Figure 5). For example, the task “Propose mechanistic models for protein–protein or protein–ligand interactions” is 5.6x more likely to appear on Claude Science than on Claude. This task involves explaining, at the level of individual molecular contacts, how a protein binds to another protein or to a small molecule such as a drug. Claude Science is well positioned for this task, as it can gather protein structures and binding data from multiple connected databases and combine them into a testable model of the interaction. By contrast, Claude Science is used relatively less for general tasks such as drafting manuscripts or coordinating with collaborators. Moreover, we find that ideation and hypothesis generation accounts for 8% of Claude Science sessions, roughly on par with the share of researcher time these tasks take, suggesting that specialized AI tools for science can facilitate more complex and creative work.

Figure 5. Claude Science use across fields and tasks. The top panel shows the top 10 fields by their share of all Claude Science research sessions. The bottom panel shows the most and least typical tasks on Claude Science relative to Claude within biology and biomedical sciences specifically, calculated as follows: (task’s share of Claude Science research sessions in these fields) / (its share of Claude research sessions in these fields).

Focusing on biomedical sciences and biology, and using measures from our economic primitives framework introduced in [prior work](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-january-2026-report) (Appel et al. 2026), we find that tasks on Claude Science are longer, more sophisticated, and performed more autonomously than those on Claude and Claude Code (Figure 6). This is consistent with specialized AI tools being used for more demanding tasks.[8](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-researchers-use-claude#footnote-8) Sessions on Claude Science contain work that would take nearly twice as many human hours to complete without AI as work on Claude and Claude Code. The content of these sessions is also more advanced. Over 80% of Claude Science use is estimated to require doctoral-level training (20 years of education), compared with about 50–70% on other interfaces. Over 70% of Claude Science sessions are performed with a high degree of AI autonomy, far higher than on Claude (23.7%) and somewhat higher than on Claude Code (64.1%).

Figure 6. Use characteristics in biology and biomedical sciences. Comparing human task time, doctoral-level content, and level of autonomy for tasks completed on Claude, Claude Code, and Claude Science in biology and biomedical sciences. The hatched orange and blue bars reweight the subfields and tasks performed by Claude and Claude Code to match those performed by Claude Science.

**Outputs of Claude use.** Finally, the artifacts produced with Claude show clear differences across fields (Figure 7).[9](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-researchers-use-claude#footnote-9) Written artifacts such as documents, reports, or academic papers are more common in the social sciences and humanities, relative to the natural and formal sciences. Conversely, technical artifacts such as code, software outputs, and data are more frequent in the natural and formal sciences. The health sciences overall are more similar to the social sciences in terms of artifacts produced. Technical artifacts are about twice as common on Claude Code as on Claude, but the broad pattern across fields is similar (see the [Appendix](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf)).

## How use relates to capabilities

Does AI usage track AI capabilities? Field size explains some of the variation in Claude use, but not all of it. For example, researchers in computer science use Claude far more than the field’s size predicts, while those in nutrition use Claude far less. Similarly, where researchers spend their own time does not fully predict where they use Claude the most. Some broad task categories, such as data analysis and writing, have a much larger share of usage than of research time, while others, such as data gathering and ideation and hypothesis generation, have a much smaller share. Here, we test whether these differences in usage reflect what AI can actually do.

To measure task-specific AI capabilities, we first construct capability scores using an LLM classifier. For each task, SciNet provides a list of individual steps that a scientist needs to perform to complete the task. For instance, in chemistry, one task involves purifying reaction mixtures, and one of that task’s steps involves packing a chromatography column. The classifier is shown pairs of steps across the taxonomy and asked which one AI can perform better. We aggregate these comparisons using a Bradley–Terry model—the same model behind the Elo ratings that rank chess players based on head-to-head results. A step’s score rises when it wins comparisons and falls when it loses them. We then average each task’s steps to obtain a task-level score (see the [Appendix](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf) for details). We label this measure _theoretical AI capability_. We refer to a task’s specific score as its _AI capability score_.

To compare usage across tasks, we ask whether Claude use is higher or lower for a task, relative to the time researchers spend on that task. For example, a task that takes 5% of researchers’ time but accounts for 10% of Claude sessions would represent twice as much use as expected, a ratio of 2. A ratio of 1 indicates that Claude use is in line with researcher time spent on the task. A ratio below 1 indicates that Claude is used less than expected based on the amount of researcher time the task takes.

Figure 8 shows that this use ratio rises sharply with a task’s AI capability score. For tasks in the lowest 20% of capability scores, like purifying reaction mixtures and conducting participant observation, Claude is barely used. At median capability scores, the ratio is roughly 1, so usage is in line with researcher time. For tasks in the top 20% of capability scores, like visualizing data and applying statistical tests, usage is about twice as high on Claude and about four times as high on Claude Code, compared with the typical time researchers spend on those tasks. These patterns suggest that scientists’ use of Claude closely tracks AI capability scores. The steeper gradient for Claude Code reflects its focus on computational work, which has higher capability scores.

Figure 8. Claude use and theoretical AI capability. The vertical axis represents the use ratio for the tasks in each capability quintile, calculated as the share of research sessions devoted to those tasks divided by their share of research time estimated from SciNet. The horizontal axis represents the task’s AI capability score (percentile). The orange dots represent the average use ratio for tasks in each AI capability quintile for Claude; the blue dots represent the same for Claude Code.

Theoretical AI capabilities can differ across tasks for many reasons. To understand these differences, we relate each task’s AI capability score to three groups of LLM-derived task characteristics: its suitability for reinforcement learning (RL); whether the work is predominantly cognitive, interpersonal, or physical; and whether regulatory restrictions limit AI use (Figure 9; see the [Appendix](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf) for definitions and data sources).

Consider RL suitability. In RL, an AI model tries a task repeatedly and is rewarded when it succeeds, allowing the model to learn how to perform that task. Tasks are particularly suitable for RL when success is easy to verify, when many examples can be simulated in a computing environment, and when feedback can be delivered quickly (Lambert et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2025). We use task-specific measures from SciNet for these three RL-related components and summarize them in a single index. A task’s capability score is highly correlated with its RL index score, which was classified separately (correlation of 0.78). This suggests that AI is most capable at the research tasks where RL is easiest to apply.

Other important task characteristics concern the nature of the work. Some research tasks are fundamentally cognitive, while others require physical actions or interpersonal contact. Physical tasks in general are beyond the reach of purely cognitive machines. There can also be regulatory restrictions that limit AI use. AI capability scores are higher for tasks characterized by cognitive work (correlation of 0.69). By contrast, research tasks that involve more physical work have lower AI capability scores (−0.57). Scores also tend to be lower for research activities that include interpersonal work (−0.31) or that involve regulatory restrictions on AI use (−0.38).

Figure 9. Theoretical AI capability and other task characteristics. Correlations between a research task’s AI capability score percentile and other characteristics of the task.

The same task characteristics also predict how much researchers use Claude for a given task (Figure 10). Claude is barely used for tasks in the bottom fifth of RL suitability. For tasks in the top fifth, Claude use is disproportionately high relative to the time researchers spend on them: twice as high on Claude, and four times as high on Claude Code. This is similar to the association between Claude use and AI capability scores overall, where the top 20% of tasks also draw two to four times the expected use.

Claude use is also disproportionately high in predominantly cognitive tasks. By contrast, Claude use is low for physical and interpersonal tasks. Claude use is also lower for tasks where regulatory barriers limit AI assistance. Overall, across the spectrum of highly specialized research tasks, usage and capability measures are strongly related.

Figure 10. Claude use and task characteristics. Each panel considers a different task characteristic. The vertical axes represent the use ratio for groups of tasks (“bins”) with different scores on that characteristic. The horizontal axes represent a measure of the characteristic’s intensity. Orange dots represent the average use ratio for tasks in each bin on Claude; blue dots represent the same for Claude Code. Dots at zero represent bins hidden for privacy because too few accounts are responsible for that use.

Different scientific fields rely on different mixes of research activities. Does the correlation between usage and capability scores for narrow research tasks help explain why some fields use Claude more than others? Our data shows it does: a subfield’s Claude use per researcher tends to rise with the average AI capability score across its tasks (correlations of 0.45 for Claude and 0.57 for Claude Code), as shown in Figure 11.

There is also substantial variation across domains, which suggests that Claude use is affected by factors beyond what AI can do. Health sciences researchers use Claude relatively little compared with the AI capability scores of their tasks, possibly because regulation of medical data and research limits the use of AI or makes it harder to integrate. Researchers in the formal sciences, social sciences, and the arts and humanities use Claude more than the AI capability scores would predict. Much of the work in these fields is done through text and code, which likely facilitates the adoption of AI tools. Claude use in the physical sciences and life sciences generally tracks AI capability scores more closely, though individual subfields can deviate.

Theoretical AI capability also predicts how much researchers allow Claude to work autonomously. Figure 12 shows that autonomous use correlates with the theoretical AI capability of the subfield. In other words, not only do subfields with more theoretical AI capability use AI more, but they also use it in a more autonomous manner.

## Research acceleration scenarios with AI

The pace of scientific and technological progress depends on good ideas and the means to pursue them. Historically, both have been limited by human capacity. There are only so many researchers, and each has only so much time to formulate a hypothesis, design the right tests, run the analyses, and synthesize the findings.

AI could ease these constraints in two ways. First, it could take on work that researchers now do, allowing them to pursue more ideas. Second, it could contribute ideas that researchers might not otherwise identify. The second may ultimately matter most, but it is much harder to measure, so our analysis focuses on the first.

### Approach

We consider three scenarios of scientific acceleration through AI. Scenario 1 assumes wide adoption of current AI capabilities, as estimated from our survey and the AI capability scores. Scenarios 2 and 3 are hypothetical futures in which AI fully automates 75% or 100% of non-physical research steps—the individual actions that make up each research task in SciNet—starting with the steps that have the highest AI capability scores. In all three scenarios, researchers continue to perform physical steps. These are illustrative scenarios, not forecasts. They show how physical work, theoretical AI capability, and the cost of AI could shape the acceleration of scientific research, and which fields could accelerate the most.

For each scenario, we report two measures. The first is the _AI share_: the fraction of researcher time spent on a study that AI could take on. Given how researchers currently allocate their time, it measures how much human research time AI could free up, and it follows directly from our analyses above. Researchers can redirect that freed-up time to other work, increasing research output. Our second measure, the _acceleration_, estimates this gain: the increase in research output for a fixed research budget, relative to the research output without AI. It tells us how many more research results the same budget could produce. Freed-up time does not translate one-for-one into more output, both because researchers redirect their effort toward the tasks where AI helps less, and because AI itself has costs.

We build the AI share for Scenario 1 from our survey, which records five levels of AI use: none, assistive, shared, delegated, and autonomous. We assume these levels correspond to AI taking on 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of a researcher’s time for a given task step. Within each task category, we rank task steps by AI capability score. We then assign a level of AI use to each task step, in the same proportions reported in our survey, with the highest levels going to the highest-scoring steps. For ideation and hypothesis generation, for example, 17% of responses report no AI use, and 53% report assistive use. The 17% of ideation task steps with the lowest AI capability scores are therefore assigned no AI share, the next 53% are assigned 25%, and so on up the scale. Because respondents are likely to be heavy AI adopters, we treat their reported use as a proxy for what AI can do today. Finally, we aggregate researcher time freed up by AI from steps to fields (see the [Appendix](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf)).

To compute the acceleration, we use an ideas production function, a standard economic model of how research inputs, such as researcher time, are turned into research outputs. In this model, a paper requires many research tasks (Jones 2026). The tasks are complementary, such that strength in one cannot fully make up for weakness in another. As AI makes some tasks cheaper, researchers shift their effort toward tasks where AI helps less, thereby easing the bottlenecks that limit the overall gain. For example, researchers could spend more time coming up with new questions or running more physical experiments to expand empirical evidence. We calibrate how much these bottlenecks limit the overall gain using Ahmadpoor and Jones (2019). For AI’s cost, we assume that an hour of AI agent time costs $30, compared with $95 for an hour of a researcher’s time, and that AI runs for 0.2 hours for each researcher hour it saves. These are illustrative assumptions: the AI cost uses today’s public list prices. The smaller these costs, the more research can be done within a given budget and the larger the acceleration, as we discuss below. The [Appendix](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/fafb3d4237f9b53fe114fef4fb5109b9cf5b5e60.pdf) describes the model and assumptions.

Circles show estimates for current capabilities; diamonds show the estimated share of time freed up if 75% of non-physical steps are automated; triangles show the estimated share of time freed up if 100% of non-physical steps are automated. The right panel shows the research acceleration given the research budget of a field. Its horizontal axis shows research output with AI as a multiple of the output without AI (1x = no change) under our three scenarios.

### Scenario 1: Current capabilities

With current capabilities, if adopted widely, AI could take on 27–52% of the researcher time needed to produce a new study, with a median of 38% across fields (Figure 13, left panel). These shares imply research accelerations ranging from 1.35x in the arts, 1.36x in anthropology, and 1.41x in chemistry to 1.96x in computer science and 2.02x in statistics (Figure 13, right panel). Accelerations remain limited because AI work today is mostly assistive or shared rather than autonomous, so researchers still spend substantial time on nearly every step. Even the most AI-ready fields, whose work is almost entirely cognitive, reach only about a 2x acceleration. These estimates assume that AI diffuses widely and that researchers apply it to every step where it is useful today.

Two factors explain most of the differences across fields. The first is physical work, which AI cannot take on without physical integrations. In lab and field sciences such as the agricultural sciences, biomedical sciences, chemistry, and materials science, 38–41% of researcher time goes to physical steps, compared with 4% in the median field. The physical share alone explains about one-third of the variation in the AI share. The second factor is the distribution of AI capability scores within cognitive work. The arts and history spend little time on physical steps (10% and 4%, respectively), yet their cognitive steps have low AI capability scores, perhaps because qualitative and interpretive work is less amenable to reinforcement learning. Computer science and statistics, by contrast, have high scores and large AI shares, driven by data analysis, the type of work with the highest AI capability scores across fields.

Fields with a higher use ratio on Claude also tend to have larger AI shares (Figure 14). The AI share is calculated without any Claude use data, so this relationship is further evidence that AI use tracks theoretical AI capability.

Figure 14. Claude use and research time freed up by AI across fields. The correlation between observed Claude use and the AI share for each field. Each field is a dot, sized by the number of researchers. The vertical axis shows the field’s use ratio on Claude, on a log scale. The horizontal axis shows the estimated share of human research time that could be completed by AI with current capabilities.

### Scenarios 2 and 3: Greater automation

In these more speculative scenarios, which depend heavily on assumptions about AI’s reach and costs, greater automation raises both the AI share and the acceleration sharply, and widens the differences between fields.

In Scenario 2, where 75% of non-physical steps can be done by AI, accelerations reach about 8x in computer science, 7x in statistics and library and information science, and 6x in economics. Most other social sciences, mathematics, and physics and astronomy reach 4–5x, while bench and medical sciences reach about 2–3x. The bench sciences and the arts and humanities gain the least, but for different reasons. In the bench sciences, much of the work is physical and must still be done by researchers. In the arts and humanities, the work is mostly cognitive but has low AI capability scores, so few of its steps are among those automated. Automated steps make up 82% of non-physical researcher time in computer science but only 28% in the arts. History, anthropology, and the arts accelerate by about 2x or less.

In Scenario 3, the arts and humanities pull ahead, while the bench sciences remain held back. Once AI automates all non-physical steps, low AI capability scores no longer limit cognitive work, and AI can take on nearly all research work in many fields (Figure 13, left panel). In this speculative scenario, fields with few physical tasks, including statistics, computer science, economics, mathematics, psychology, and most of the social sciences and the arts and humanities, reach about 15–16x. Fields with substantial lab or field work, such as biology, chemistry, materials science, agricultural sciences, and biomedical sciences, reach only 3–4x. Absent automated labs, their physical steps, representing about 40% of researcher time, remain the bottleneck.

Two lessons follow from these scenarios. First, physical bottlenecks remain powerful. Steps that AI cannot perform without physical integrations come to dominate researcher time and budgets, capping acceleration. Second, where no such bottlenecks exist, extraordinary rates of progress become possible, and AI’s cost becomes the limit. Under our assumptions, a task that is fully automated by AI still costs about one-sixteenth of what it would cost a researcher to perform, so accelerations approach about 16x and stop there. Lower AI costs would raise this ceiling proportionately, such that halving the cost of AI, for example, would double the rate of progress. If costs fall sharply, including for harder tasks that AI automates later, the fields without physical bottlenecks could accelerate far beyond these estimates.

These calculations have important limits that cut in both directions. We do not consider constraints at the level of a field rather than a research team. For instance, only so many experiments can run at facilities like the Large Hadron Collider. We also count only the cost of researcher and AI time, not the costs of machinery or inputs for experiments, which can be substantial in the health, physical, and life sciences. Nor do we consider simulations to replace physical experiments, which could ease bottlenecks in some fields. Finally, we do not consider the quality of ideas. If AI identifies better hypotheses with higher chances of success, research could accelerate further; if research taste remains uniquely human, the acceleration would be slower.

### Researcher expectations

The scenarios above characterize a range of possibilities. Researchers’ own expectations can provide a unique guide grounded in expertise. To gather their viewpoints, our survey of researchers in the life and health sciences asked when they thought AI, working on its own, would match the best researchers in their field for different categories of research work (Figure 15). Respondents’ expectations are high in the near term: over half expect AI to match top researchers at data analysis by 2027, and 38% say that AI has already reached this level. By 2030, 94% expect AI will match the best researchers in data analysis, 89% in writing, and 72% each in ideation and data gathering. Expectations for ideation and data gathering are lower, consistent with the lower use of Claude for these tasks today.

Expectations for end-to-end AI research without humans in the loop are more conservative, but researchers still expect remarkable progress. About 40% of respondents expect AI to carry out full research projects by 2030, and about 64% by 2035, although 16% say it will never happen. Expectations that AI will autonomously produce scientific breakthroughs are more cautious still, with approximately half of researchers expecting this to occur only after 2035, if ever. Overall, many researchers expect high levels of automation in the next decade, broadly in line with our greater automation scenarios. How fast and to what extent these expectations materialize will depend not only on advances in AI as a software technology, but also on efforts to build automated labs that overcome physical barriers.

Comparing past survey evidence to realized outcomes suggests that AI progress has often been dramatically underestimated (Rosenberg et al. 2026). In a survey in mid-2022, for example, the median estimate for AI achieving gold-level performance in the International Mathematical Olympiad was 2030 among domain experts and 2035 among superforecasters (Karger et al. 2023). AI achieved this milestone in 2025, some five to 10 years ahead of those predictions. While forecasters have systematically underestimated progress in AI capabilities, there are a few exceptions, primarily in domains that require execution in the physical world, such as AI-assisted laboratory tasks in biology.

## Discussion

If AI helps speed up scientific discovery, it could contribute to higher standards of living and faster progress in treating disease. Our estimates suggest the potential gains are substantial. But advancements in AI’s research capabilities do not automatically translate into rapid progress; achieving such acceleration will also depend on how we respond to several constraints.

First, adoption can substantially lag capabilities. Diffusion of new technologies is typically slow (Comin and Hobijn 2010), and productivity gains tend to materialize only after organizations restructure around them (David 1990; Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson 2021). Individual learning processes, friction at the organizational or system level, and constraints on AI access or infrastructure can all slow adoption.

Second, research may continue to face bottlenecks that AI does not remove. Physical experimentation, which can be costly and slow, can substantially limit progress even with extremely capable AI. We see this especially in the bench sciences in the scenarios above. Choosing what to study may be another bottleneck. Research questions largely determine a research project’s value, and AI’s capacity to generate good questions remains unclear. If ideation is a bottleneck, it will limit acceleration even as answering a given question becomes much cheaper. Conversely, if AI outstrips humans in formulating high-quality hypotheses, acceleration could be more substantial, especially in domains where testing hypotheses is costly.

Third, AI-enabled research could raise the quantity or quality of research output, with different implications for science. A surge in new research papers could advance a field faster but also overwhelm scientists’ capacity to absorb or verify the results. On the other hand, new technologies, from microscopes and telescopes to statistical software, have historically raised the quality of scientific insights, and AI can likewise make more comprehensive analyses and more ambitious projects feasible. And as papers become easier to produce, the research community may increasingly reward work that is especially novel or high quality. Whether more efficient research leads to greater quantity or greater quality will depend on researchers’ choices and the incentives set by journals, funders, and institutions.

Fourth, the way research is organized may change in ways that are hard to anticipate. As AI becomes more capable and autonomous, AI systems could take the lead in verification, refereeing, and communicating important results, which would ease the absorption problem discussed above. Further in the future, AI research agents might also push the frontiers of science in large communities of their own, independently of humans, using robotics and automated labs. Such autonomous AI research would raise questions about how humans keep track of new knowledge, whether they can understand it, and how it is generated.

Fifth, more autonomous AI systems may increase the challenge of ensuring that AI-driven research remains aligned with human goals. Keeping humans involved in key research decisions, such as which questions to pursue and how to pursue them, may matter for safety and human wellbeing as AI agents become more powerful and creative. Discoveries themselves, or the byproducts of research, can also carry risks, particularly in biology, where the same capabilities that speed up medical advances could also produce dangerous knowledge or materials. In such cases, deliberate checkpoints in the research process may be warranted, requiring us to accept slower progress to reduce risk.

Finally, the work that researchers undertake may change substantially. One effect may be a change in researchers’ job satisfaction. If AI takes over enjoyable or rewarding tasks, satisfaction may fall and fewer people may choose research careers. If AI instead takes over tasks that researchers find tedious, satisfaction may rise. Administrative work is one potential target. In the US, principal investigators on federally funded projects report spending about 44% of their research time meeting administrative requirements rather than doing research (Schneider et al. 2020), and easing this burden could be a large boon, saving time and improving overall satisfaction. Ultimately, the net effect is uncertain and may change as AI’s capabilities evolve. An optimistic view is that AI will expand opportunities for discovery, which is core to what makes research careers rewarding. If science is a voyage into the “endless frontier,” AI may be a new vessel, allowing researchers to travel farther across a vast expanse of research questions and discoveries.

A. Usage sample
Our analysis is based on two samples of Claude session transcripts that involve
scientific work from Chat and Cowork (henceforth labeled Claude for
simplicity) and Claude Code. In a supplemental analysis, we also study a
sample of sessions on Claude Science. The sample covers accounts on
consumer plans (Free, Pro, and Max plans) from August 2 to September 30,
2026. For Claude Code, we include only sessions in which a person works with
Claude interactively and exclude programmatic usage.

All analyses in this report are conducted through a privacy-preserving analysis
pipeline. All classifications are done exclusively by classifiers that use language
models: each session’s transcript is shown to a language model together with a
classification prompt, and the model assigns a label from a closed list of
options. All numbers in this report are reported only for cells that pass a
minimum number of accounts.

The sample is built in three steps in the same way for each product. First, we
sample a set of session transcripts from each of the Claude products analyzed
in this study. Second, we use a classifier to determine whether a session
transcript involves scientific or research work, judged by the activity in the
transcript. This yields 148,734 session transcripts for Claude and 86,884 for
Claude Code. Third, a classifier assigns each of the session transcripts to one
subfield and one task according to the SciNet taxonomy described in the next
section. The classifier successfully identifies a subfield and task for 94% of
scientific Claude and Claude Code session transcripts. The remaining
transcripts are too unspecific to be placed and are left out.

Our analysis covers session transcripts on core research tasks, which are tasks
performed by scientists to produce new research findings and papers (see the
description of the SciNet taxonomy in the next section for details). The
remaining tasks, which we label non-research tasks, such as teaching or service,
are not tied to producing new findings or research papers. The sample of session
transcripts covering core research tasks, which we use as our main analysis
sample, contains 128,914 sessions on Claude and 78,135 on Claude Code.

Because we sample a constant number (as opposed to a constant share) of
session transcripts per hour and product, the share of usage sampled varies across products and time. All analyses therefore weight each session transcript
by the inverse of its sampling probability: the total number of session
transcripts in its product and hour, divided by the number of sampled
transcripts in that same product and hour.

While our analysis focuses on core research tasks, non-research tasks can take
up a substantial part of a researcher’s time budget. In a survey of academic
researchers, the estimates of Schneider et al. (2020) suggest that such tasks
take up approximately two-thirds of researcher time, while SciNet estimates
63.4%. Our scientific usage classifier intentionally leaves aside undergraduate
teaching and does not select administrative or routine work if it is not serving
research or a study directly. Furthermore, our sample is not restricted to
scientists in academic positions, who have higher teaching and service
workloads. As a result, only 7.7% of sessions on Claude and 4.2% on Claude
Code are mapped to non-research work, which is substantially lower than
academic scientists’ time allocation on these tasks.

B. SciNet taxonomy
SciNet is a dataset describing researcher tasks across all domains of science. It
comprises a hierarchy of 6 domains, 34 fields within those domains, 318
subfields, 7,262 tasks, and 90,583 task steps (Alamos and Althoff 2026). This
hierarchy structures our analysis, helping us map sessions that researchers
have with Claude to the work that their process of scientific discovery requires.
Beyond the hierarchical field and task taxonomy, SciNet contains rich detail on
various aspects of scientific work, including time researchers spend on each
task and whether regulatory restrictions affect the use of AI in a given task.

SciNet is built by combining large language models with established survey
designs from O*NET, a widely studied taxonomy of labor market tasks built
from human expert interviews. SciNet is validated using data from thousands of
academic papers, research protocols from wet-lab sciences, human expert
feedback, and human ratings for scientific tasks on O*NET. Using large
language models to construct SciNet allows for a level of detail that would be
prohibitively expensive or logistically infeasible with human expert input alone.

SciNet is licensed under CC BY 4.0 (link). We aggregated and combined SciNet
data with our own measures, as described in this Appendix.

Details. SciNet starts out by building on a taxonomy of 26 fields as defined by
OpenAlex, a database of global research outputs including papers and
preprints. It then uses human expert input to merge and split those into 34
refined fields and group them into six domains. SciNet then uses large
language models to propose typical subfields for each field and sorts
OpenAlex’s research topics into them.

Having established a field hierarchy that links to OpenAlex’s academic output,
SciNet then defines tasks through language models and human review. It
begins with a large language model proposing and the authors curating 30
universal tasks, shared by researchers across all fields, and 69 domain-specific
tasks. It then asks a large language model to suggest tasks specific to subfields,
which refines domain tasks. Around one-fifth are later judged as trivial and
therefore removed. All task descriptions closely follow O*NET’s task
description style. SciNet then validates all tasks by drawing around 100 papers
per subfield and recording which task each paper performs. For work that is
not covered by existing tasks, new tasks are proposed. New tasks are kept only
if at least two papers propose them and at least 5% of papers perform them.
Finally, all tasks are broken into steps, for which various characteristics are
computed (see the section below). These steps are validated using public
protocols from protocols.io, where wet-lab scientists record rich information on
their experiments’ steps and researcher time allocated to them.

Example
•    Domain: Life Sciences
•    Field: Biology
•    Subfield: Evolutionary Biology
•    Task: Conduct phylogenetic analyses to infer evolutionary
relationships among taxa.
•    Step: Run a maximum likelihood tree search in IQ-TREE (or RAxML) under the
selected partitioned models, specifying an appropriate outgroup to root the tree.

Task characteristics

In addition to the taxonomy that links specific researcher tasks to a given
subfield, SciNet contains information on characteristics of those tasks and
their steps. When a task is relevant to multiple subfields, these characteristics
are usually specific to a task-subfield combination.

The task characteristics we use are:

•    Hierarchical position. SciNet distinguishes universal tasks that are shared
by all fields, tasks specific to a domain, tasks specific to fields, and tasks
specific to subfields.
•    Universal tasks (0.4% of tasks, e.g., “Apply statistical methods to test
hypotheses and estimate effect sizes”)
•    Domain-specific tasks (1.8% of tasks, e.g., “Formulate hypotheses about
biological mechanisms, pathways, or evolutionary processes”)
•    Field-specific tasks (4.4% of tasks, e.g., “Identify proteins from mass
spectrometry data by matching spectra to reference sequences” in the
field of biology)
•    Subfield-specific tasks (93.3% of tasks, e.g., “Analyze differences in
microbial growth rates, gene expression, or metabolic outputs between
treatment groups” in the subfield of microbiology)
•    Task category. Tasks are grouped into categories:
•    Reading and knowledge acquisition (2.0% of tasks)
•    Ideation and hypothesis generation (7.4% of tasks)
•    Data gathering (40.8% of tasks)
•    Data analysis (42.9% of tasks)
•    Theoretical analysis (2.5% of tasks)
•    Design and development (2.2% of tasks)
•    Writing and communication (0.3% of tasks)
•    Peer review and service (0.1% of tasks)
•    Mentorship and teaching (0.2% of tasks)

•    Research versus non-research tasks. Core research tasks are those that are
done to generate new scientific findings and that typically result in an
academic paper, including ideation, data gathering, data analysis, theoretical
analysis, and writing. Non-research tasks are those that are done by researchers for goals not directly related to their own academic output, such as teaching,
mentorship, and service. In our analysis, we focus on core research tasks.

Format: Binary variable specific to task × subfield combinations. 23,543
research task × subfield combinations, 3,260 non-research task × subfield
combinations. Assigned by LLM.

•    Researcher hours per task. SciNet estimates the hours the average
researcher in a given subfield spends on a given research task over the course
of a year. Time estimates are generated first at the granular step level and
then aggregated to the task level. With the time per task instance in hand,
assessing overall researcher time spent on a task per year requires additional
information on the annual number of task instances. SciNet estimates this in
two steps: the number of instances per paper, and the annual number of
papers per researcher. The latter estimate is derived from OpenAlex, a
database on hundreds of millions of research papers that allows computing
per-researcher counts for each subfield. It assigns fractional authorship to
each coauthor. Total hours spent on each task are then the hours per instance
multiplied by instances per paper and papers per year.

Format: Hours specific to task × subfield combinations. Computed as a
combination of paper counts by subfield on OpenAlex (2015–2019) and
LLM-based time estimates validated via (1) time recorded on research
protocols for wet-lab sciences from protocols.io and (2) the number of papers
that contain a task, classified using an LLM and full text from 31,500
open-access papers on OpenAlex (2000–2020) as a proxy for task frequency.

•    Regulatory restrictions. This measure captures the degree to which
researchers face regulatory barriers in using AI assistance for a given task. It
is scored on a scale from 0 to 4 and is based on Parshall and Lopez-Luzuriaga
(2026). The scale ranks regulatory barriers from least to most binding, with 0
for none, 1 for social or market norms, 2 for professional standards or liability,
3 for statute, and 4 for tasks that require a personally accountable human,
such as giving sworn testimony or consent. Each task’s steps are rated
separately, and a task is assigned the average value across all steps
(weighting by steps’ time share).

Format: Score from 0 to 4 specific to each task (sometimes we rank the
underlying values to range from 0 to 100 instead). Assigned by LLM.

•    Suitability for reinforcement learning. This measure captures the extent to
which a task is amenable to reinforcement learning (RL). RL environments
have been an important part of recent progress in AI capabilities. In an RL
environment, an AI model attempts a problem, receives feedback on its
attempt, and uses that feedback to improve. Problems tend to be suitable for
RL when they (1) are verifiable, (2) are simulatable, and (3) have high feedback density, three key dimensions in Tomei and Klein Teeselink’s
(2026) rubric for RL feasibility. Verifiability ranges from low (e.g., requiring
contested human expert judgment) to high (fully automatic check).
Simulatability ranges from low (requiring real people and real stakes) to high
(fully digital and easy to replicate). Feedback density ranges from rare
(delayed and expensive signals) to continuous (immediate and cheap). Each
dimension is rated by an LLM at the step level and then aggregated to the
task level by averaging across the relevant steps (weighting by steps’ time
share). In a principal component analysis (PCA), the three dimensions are
then summarized into an RL index that explains 79.5% of the variance across
those variables.

Format: Scores for each dimension from 1 to 10 specific to each task,
aggregated into a principal component with mean 0 and standard deviation 1
across tasks (sometimes we rank the underlying values to range from 0 to 100
instead). Assigned by LLM.

In addition to task characteristics from SciNet, we use additional
characteristics created with assistance from large language models:

•    Theoretical AI capability and AI capability scores. The AI capability score
considers how well current AI capabilities can perform each task, relative to
other tasks, through pairwise comparisons of the underlying SciNet research
steps. We construct this measure as follows: a judge model is shown two
SciNet steps, each with its task and the associated subfield, field, or domain it
belongs to. Given the two steps, the judge chooses the step for which AI’s
typical output without human help would come closer to what an
experienced researcher in that field would accept without substantial
correction. AI is defined as an agent on a computer that uses the best publicly
available general and specialist AI systems, communicates with people only
in writing, and has no physical or laboratory integration available. Steps are
paired over eight rounds, two rounds with random pairings and then six
rounds with steps of similar rating. A Bradley–Terry model fitted to the
317,560 comparisons gives each step an ordered rating. The judge can also
state that AI cannot do a step at all. The 16% of steps that are judged this way
in both of their first two comparisons are placed below all others. Because the
comparisons identify the order of the ratings better than the distances
between them, we express each step’s rating as its percentile among all rated
steps. A given task takes the mean percentile of its steps, and our task
measure is the percentile rank of that mean across all tasks (100 = most
capable). If the same task has a different set of steps in different fields, that
task will have a separate rank and percentile for each field or subfield in
which it appears. For example, the universal SciNet task “Acquire or access
primary data sources relevant to research questions” has very different steps
in different fields.

The theoretical capability measure is similar in objective to the exposure
measure of Eloundou et al. (2024). Both use an LLM judge to assess how well
AI can perform specific tasks. Eloundou et al. (2024) used GPT-4 to place
each O*NET task in one of three categories, based on whether a language
model could cut the time it takes by at least half. We use pairwise
comparisons rather than categorical ratings for two reasons. First, there is no
given absolute scale for how well AI can perform a given research step.
Where no such scale exists, the literature suggests that comparative
judgments are typically more accurate than absolute judgments for both
human judges (Mantiuk, Tomaszewska, and Mantiuk 2012; Shah et al. 2014)
and LLM judges (Liu et al. 2024). Second, the AI capability score ranks tasks
far more finely. The AI capability score correlates with the broad task
exposure labels of Eloundou et al. (2024) when we apply their rubric to
SciNet tasks, but the AI capability score distinguishes between the tasks that
their rubric places within the same category. For example, 58% of tasks are
labeled as “directly exposed” according to the Eloundou et al. (2024) rubric
but with no further distinction between them.

Format: Percentile AI rank from 0 to 100 for each task (computed separately
for each field or subfield where the task’s steps differ). Assigned by LLM.

•    Cognitive, physical, and interpersonal work. A language model is shown
each SciNet task step, with its task and associated subfield, field, and domain,
and assigns the step to exactly one of three categories. The categories are
defined as material things worked on with one’s own body (physical),
information and ideas (cognitive), or people, through any medium
(interpersonal). A step with any physical component is classified as physical.
Only steps without any physical component are classified as cognitive or
interpersonal, whichever dominates. At the task level, the measure is the
share of its steps in each category, with each step counting equally.

Format: Percentage of a task’s steps in each category, summing to 100%
(computed separately for each field and subfield where a given task’s steps
differ). Assigned by LLM.

Researcher counts

SciNet measures the number of active researchers in a given field and subfield
using data on hundreds of millions of working papers and publications from
OpenAlex. First, the corpus on OpenAlex is restricted to active authors with at
least five papers in total—at least one before 2020 and at least one since 2020.
Second, SciNet randomly samples 746,517 researchers (sampled separately in
each country) and classifies each researcher’s subfield based on the titles, journals, and years of up to 40 of their papers. The main classifier is Claude
Haiku 5.5, validated with 1,500 classifications from Sonnet 5.5, which is given
each paper’s abstract as additional information (85% agreement on field, 74%
agreement on subfield). Lastly, the number of researchers is estimated as a
subfield’s share of researchers determined in the previous step, times the total
number of researchers in a given country, and summed over countries.

For further information on SciNet, see https://github.com/lukasalthoff/scinet.

C. Classifying Claude sessions
We use a privacy-preserving analysis pipeline in which model-based classifiers
are provided with a session transcript and a prompt and asked to assign labels
such as a field and task from a closed-form response rubric. Results are
reported only in aggregate. For a given cell, estimates are only reported if they
pass a minimum number of accounts.

Classifier overview

We use five classifiers to characterize scientific Claude sessions:

•    Scientific work screener: An initial classifier that identifies whether a given
session transcript covers scientific use. Only transcripts that involve
scientific use are reviewed and classified by all other downstream classifiers.
•    Scientific field and task classifier: Identifies the domain, field, and subfield
of a given scientific transcript. Then, the classifier identifies the task being
worked on among the tasks of the subfield picked in the previous step. The
classifier can pick any of the universal, domain-specific, field-specific, or
subfield-specific tasks relevant to the particular subfield. The classifier also
returns a confidence level between 1 and 5 for the chosen subfield and task.
•    Claude contribution classifier: Rates the contribution of Claude relative to
the task picked by the scientific field and task classifier. Contribution can
vary from “none” to “generated” (Claude completed the task’s work itself).
•    Memory-based role and career stage classifier: Identifies only the scientific
role and career stage using only explicit statements in the memory block.
This classifier is only used for Claude, and not other surfaces.
•    Memory-based scientific field classifier: Identifies only the scientific field of
the researcher using only explicit statements in the memory block. This
classifier is only used for Claude, and not other surfaces.

In addition, we use data from additional classifiers that we introduced in Appel
et al. (2026) and Massenkoff et al. (2026), for which more detail is provided
below under “Supplementary classifiers.” Preprocessing. Before a session transcript is passed to a classifier, it is reduced
to the parts the classifier should read and shortened to a fixed maximum
length. For the screener, the field and task classifier, and the contribution
classifier, we remove the system prompt. Each turn is limited to 10,000
characters, and the transcript as a whole is limited to 100,000 characters. If it is
longer, we keep whole turns from both ends, alternating between the first and
the last remaining turn. The two memory-based classifiers see no part of the
conversation. For these, we extract only the memory block, up to 100,000
characters, and a transcript without a memory block is recorded as such.

Validation of classifiers

When no ground truth is available, validation of any classifier is inherently
difficult. Without a ground-truth mapping from session transcripts to scientific
fields or tasks, we inspect the validity of our classifiers through multiple
robustness exercises and quality metrics.

Validation on synthetic session transcripts

We assess the accuracy of our field and task classifier on 5,000 synthetic session
transcripts for which we know the correct subfield and task label by construction. Synthetic transcripts are generated as follows: for each of the 318
subfields we randomly sample about 16 tasks belonging to this subfield, drawn
from its universal, domain-level, field-level, and subfield-level tasks. A first
model call turns each of the 5,000 subfield-task combinations into a description
of a concrete research situation. A second model call then converts the research
situation into a synthetic session transcript without seeing the label. Finally, a
third model call removes subfield mentions and task wording where present.
The output is a synthetic session transcript with a subfield and task label.

Using the resulting synthetic session transcripts and labels, we then evaluate
the accuracy of our classifier, which is 92% (domain), 83% (field), 74%
(subfield), 69% (task), 63% (task × field), and 58% (task × subfield). For
comparison, drawing a subfield and one of its tasks at random would be correct
in 20% of cases for the domain, 3% for the field, 0.3% for the subfield, 0.5% for
the task, and less than 0.1% for the two combinations. About half of the
transcripts still echo some of the label’s wording after the rewrite. Excluding
them lowers accuracy to 64% (task) and 54% (task × subfield), while field and
subfield accuracy barely change.

Comparison of results based on transcript-based classification and
memory-based classification

Our main results measure use across scientific fields based on transcript
classifications (i.e., based on the content of sessions). As an alternative, we
could measure use based on the field of the researcher, which is typically not
observed. For a subset of users, however, we can infer a field of research by
having a classifier identify this information based on Claude’s memory. This
gives us two separate signals of the researcher’s field, one based on the content
of the session and one based on the memory.

As a validation check for our field classifier, we use a sample of Claude session
transcripts where we have memory available. We then correlate the share of
transcripts in a given field based on the transcript-based classifier with the
share of transcripts in a given field based on the memory-based classifier.
These shares do not need to show a correlation of 1, even if classifiers have
100% accuracy, because of interdisciplinary use: researchers in one field may
conduct interdisciplinary research in another field, which would lead to differences in the memory- and transcript-based field counts. We find a log-log
correlation of 0.92.

Robustness

We perform two additional robustness analyses with regard to our classifier
results. We assess (1) the role of potential classification error in mapping
sessions to SciNet tasks and fields, and (2) the role of sessions in which Claude

First, we analyze the subsample of sessions for which our classifier maps a
transcript to a SciNet task and field with high confidence. The classifier emits a
confidence score from 1 to 5 for both the task and the field. We construct a
high-confidence sample by restricting to sessions for which both scores are 4
or 5. This sample contains 62% of research usage of the main sample for

Possible directions for future refinements of
our task classifier

Our task classifier assigns each session to a single research task. Better
understanding how AI integrates into extended workflows may require a more fine-grained disaggregation of those sessions. In particular, future work could
explore how sessions with Claude transition across research tasks, assisted by
shared context and understanding. To support this, future versions of our
classifier could assign multiple tasks to a session. We leave this extension for
future research.

E. Technical details on methods
and analysis

Use ratios

We compute task-specific use ratios by comparing the volume of Claude use on
a specific research task to the typical time researchers spend completing that
task (based on SciNet). We define overuse versus underuse as the share of
Claude sessions specific to a given task being larger versus smaller than the
same task’s share of typical research time.

Formally, let each researcher i ∈ Is in subfield s ∈ S = {1,...,318} work on tasks
t ∈ Ts for ht,s hours per year, where Ts is the set of core research tasks in subfield s and T is the union of these sets across subfields. Let the number of
researchers in a subfield be denoted by Ns.

Typical time spent. The share of time researchers spend on a given task across
scientific fields can be computed as xt = ( Σs Ns · ht,s ) / ( Σt Σs Ns · ht,s ).

Claude use. We compute the observed Claude use for task t for product g ∈
{Claude, Claude Code} as the number of research sessions classified as
corresponding to the task. The number of sessions is weighted to correct for
differences in sampling rates across products and hours. Use shares are
computed as the number of sessions with task t on product g as a share of the
overall number of sessions on product g.

Claude use ratios. We define the use ratio for task t on product g as the use
share of sessions on product g divided by its share of typical research time, xt . A
ratio above 1 means that the task receives more use than its share of research
time implies, and a ratio below 1 means less.

Binning by task characteristics. A task’s characteristic y can differ across the
subfields in which a task is performed. We assign each task the mean of its
characteristic over those subfields, weighted by the total time researchers
spend on it in each subfield:

yt = ( Σs yt,s · ht,s · Ns ) / ( Σs Ns · ht,s ).

We then sort tasks into bins by yt . A bin’s use ratio is the sum of its tasks’ use
shares divided by the sum of their time shares xt .

Aggregation of task-level characteristics at the subfield
and field level

For several analyses, we aggregate task-level characteristics, such as AI
capability, to the subfield and field level. In both cases, each task is weighted by
the time researchers spend on it. For subfield-level aggregation, we take the
average of the characteristic across all tasks performed in the subfield,
weighted by the hours a researcher in that subfield spends on each task per
year. For field-level aggregation, we take the weighted average across all task-subfield combinations in the field. The weight of each combination is the
number of researchers in the subfield times the hours each of them spends on
the task, so larger subfields in terms of researcher hours count for more.

AI share of research work by field

We estimate how much researcher time AI can take on in producing a paper, given
how researchers report using AI today. We estimate the AI share as the research
time freed step by step, which is then aggregated to tasks, subfields, and fields.

Research time by step. SciNet estimates the hours per year that researchers in
each subfield spend on each task. As in our other analysis, we keep core
research tasks aimed at generating new knowledge, such as data gathering,
analysis, and writing, and drop non-research tasks such as teaching and
refereeing. We split each task’s hours across its steps using per-step hours
estimated by a language model (Claude Opus 5.5). Each subfield has many
tasks, and each task has many steps. A step’s weight ωj is its share of the
subfield’s core research hours: its task’s share of those hours times the step’s
share of the task’s hours. Weights therefore sum to one within a subfield.

Research time freed for each step. Estimates for researcher time freed draw on
our survey of researchers. For each of four kinds of research work (ideation,
data gathering, data analysis, and writing), respondents reported a distribution
of how they used AI on their past few papers: none, assistive, shared, delegated,
or autonomous. We treat these descriptors as saving 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and
100% of researcher time, respectively. Each step joins one of the four groups
according to its task’s SciNet category. We further assign design and
development tasks to ideation, theoretical analysis to data analysis, and
reading and knowledge acquisition to writing. The remaining research tasks,
which include tasks such as coordinating with collaborators, are assigned the
average of the four survey distributions.

Within each task group we assume that researchers use AI most on the steps AI
performs best. We pool the steps of all fields, counting a step that appears in
several fields once, and rank them by the AI capability score described in the
previous sections. Steps the judge consistently rates impossible for AI, and the
few steps without a rating, are tied at the bottom. We then assign levels of AI
use from the bottom up, so that the share of steps at each level matches the share of respondents reporting it. In ideation, for example, the lowest-rated 17%
of steps save nothing, and the next 53% by the AI capability score save 25%.
Physical and interpersonal steps are ranked like all others.

Researcher time freed by field. A subfield’s time freed, its AI share, is S=∑jωj σj ,
where ωj is the time-weight for a given step when performed without AI and σj is
the share of researcher time freed when using AI. This measure leaves out the
cost of AI and the time AI takes, so it should be interpreted as human time freed
for a given project. (In the research acceleration analysis, AI cost and time are
included.) A field’s value is the average over its subfields, weighted by their
numbers of researchers. Across the 34 fields, time freed ranges from 27% (arts) to
52% (statistics), with a median of 38%. A field will see relatively high time freed if
it has mostly non-physical steps and those steps have high AI capability scores.

Research acceleration estimates by field

To estimate potential accelerations for each research subfield and field, we
incorporate the empirical findings into an ideas production function. The
framework follows Jones (2026) with extensions to allow (i) different research
steps to take different amounts of time and (ii) researchers and AI to work
together on a given step. The next subsection develops the formal framework.
The following subsection describes the empirical implementation.

Framework

The ideas production function. Let Ż be a subfield’s rate of research output,
such as papers per year or a quality-weighted rate of papers, and let Z be the
total prior output. Research is a set of steps j whose outputs rj combine as

\[
\dot Z=\zeta Z^{\phi}\left[\sum_j\alpha_j r_j^{\theta}\right]^{1/\theta},\quad \theta<0 \tag{1}
\]

where rj is the output of research step j, ζ > 0 is a scale parameter, ϕ determines
how output depends on the cumulative stock of prior work Z, and the step
weights αj > 0 sum to 1. Because θ < 0, steps are complements, and a step that
remains hard becomes a bottleneck. Steps can also be grouped into tasks. If steps
combine within each task and tasks combine within a research project with the same θ, then the result is again (1). We assume a common θ in the analysis, so
none of the results below depend on how steps are grouped into tasks.

Producing each step. Each step’s output can be produced in different ways: by
researchers alone, by researchers and AI working together in some
combination, or by AI alone if the step becomes fully automated. A step’s
output is measured in completed instances of that step, such as a dataset
cleaned or a section drafted. Working alone, a researcher completes an instance
of step j in TL0j hours. If Lj researcher-hours are applied, then output is

\[
r_j=H_jL_j,\quad H_j=1/T^{L}_{0j} \tag{2}
\]

where Hj is the researcher productivity at the step. With AI, an instance
requires TL1j < TL0j researcher-hours, which may include instructing, checking,
and fixing the AI’s work, plus TAI
1j
hours of AI running time. An AI-assisted
instance may also have greater value, producing qj instances of researcher
output. Setting qj = 1 means working with AI produces work of the same quality
as researchers working alone. Combining Lj researcher-hours and Kj AI-hours,
the step output is

\[
r_j=q_j\min\left\{L_j/T^{L}_{1j},\ K_j/T^{AI}_{1j}\right\} \tag{3}
\]

If AI fully automates the step, TL1j = 0 and output depends on AI
time alone, rj = qj Kj / TAI
1j
.

The cost advantage. A researcher-hour costs w (the wage, common across
steps) and an AI-hour costs μ. By (2), a unit of a step’s output produced by
researchers alone costs w/Hj = w · TL0j . By (3), a unit produced with AI costs
pAI
j
= (w · TL1j + μ · TAI
1j
)/qj , which includes any researcher time the step still needs.
The cost advantage of AI at step j is then

\[
c_j=\frac{w/H_j}{p_j^{AI}} \tag{4}
\]

Researchers pick the cheaper method, so a human alone performs the step when
cj < 1. The cost for producing one unit of the step’s output is pj = min{w/Hj , pAI
j
}.

The research problem. Given an R&D budget D, researchers choose step
outputs to maximize their research output. Researchers solve

\[
\max_{\{r_j\}}\left[\sum_j\alpha_jr_j^{\theta}\right]^{1/\theta}\quad\text{subject to }\sum_j p_jr_j=D \tag{5}
\]

Let b ≡ θ/(θ − 1). Let ωj be step j’s share of the budget at the optimum.

Solving (5) gives

\[
\omega_j=\frac{\alpha_j^{1-b}p_j^b}{\sum_k\alpha_k^{1-b}p_k^b} \tag{6}
\]

Before AI, researchers perform every step, so pj = w/Hj , and the
common wage cancels:

\[
\omega_j=\frac{\alpha_j^{1-b}H_j^{-b}}{\sum_k\alpha_k^{1-b}H_k^{-b}} \tag{7}
\]

Therefore, a step takes a larger share of the initial budget, and of researcher
time, when it matters more for output (higher αj) or when researchers are less
productive at it (lower Hj). The effect of Hj reflects complementarity. Because
each step is needed, researchers cannot avoid steps where their productivity is
low. Rather, a lower Hj (a harder step) raises the researcher time devoted to that
step and consequently that step’s share of the budget.

Time freed and AI running time. It is convenient to express the times in (2)
and (3) relative to the researcher’s original time. Define

\[
\sigma_j=1-\frac{T^L_{1j}}{T^L_{0j}},\quad\tau_j=\frac{T^{AI}_{1j}}{T^L_{0j}} \tag{8}
\]

Here σj is the share of researcher time freed, and τj is the AI’s running time
relative to the researcher’s original time.

The AI share. The first of the two measures we report is the AI share: the
fraction of baseline researcher time that AI could free. This is the time freed at
each step, weighted by that step’s share of researcher time,

\[
S=\sum_j\omega_j\sigma_j \tag{9}
\]

The AI share is a direct accounting of researcher time freed.

The research acceleration. The second measure, the research acceleration, is
the resulting gain in research output for a fixed budget, after researchers
rebalance their effort and pay for AI. We derive this acceleration result next.

Proposition 1 (acceleration). (i) At the optimum, Ż = ζ · Zϕ · D/P, where P is the
cost of a unit of combined research output. (ii) If AI multiplies each step’s unit
cost by p̂ j , then, holding Z fixed, the rate of research output (Ż) per R&D dollar
increases by a factor

\[
A=\left[\sum_j\omega_j\hat p_j^b\right]^{-1/b} \tag{10}
\]

where ωj are the budget shares before the change. (iii) Because researchers
are paid the same wage w across research areas, ωj is also step j’s initial share
of research time.

We call A the research acceleration. This is how many times as much research
output can be produced per unit of research expenditure. This is the immediate
acceleration, given the current state of knowledge, Z. Over the longer run, the
knowledge stock Z will increase, which can further change the rate of progress.
For example, if there is a “fishing out” effect, where the good or easy ideas are
found earlier, then the research acceleration would decline with time.

Part (iii) of the proposition is what makes the theory measurable. Given the
degree of complementarity θ, the proposition reduces the empirical estimation
to two central objects. The first is how researchers spend their time across
steps. These are the baseline time shares ωj . These time shares absorb the step
weights αj and productivities Hj , so neither needs to be measured separately.
The second is how much AI cuts the cost of each step, p̂ j . Where AI is used,
p̂ j = 1/cj , which depends on the researcher time AI frees, the time AI itself runs,
the price of AI relative to the wage, and any gain in quality.

Cost changes in measurable terms. Substituting (2), (3), and (8) into (4), the
cost advantage of AI at step j is

\[
c_j=\frac{q_j}{(1-\sigma_j)+(\mu/w)\tau_j} \tag{11}
\]

Researchers use AI where cj ≥ 1, so AI changes step j’s unit cost by

\[
\hat p_j=\min\left\{1,\frac{(1-\sigma_j)+(\mu/w)\tau_j}{q_j}\right\} \tag{12}
\]

Substituting (12) into (10) gives the research acceleration in terms of
measurable quantities: the baseline time shares ωj , the share of researcher time
freed σj , AI running time τj , the relative price of AI μ/w, and quality qj . The
empirical exercise therefore needs these quantities step by step, applying (12)
to every step. Where researchers still do a step alone, (12) gives p̂ j = 1, so that
step’s cost is unchanged.

The acceleration as cost per paper. By Proposition 1(i), the research
acceleration has a direct interpretation. With AI, a paper of a given value costs
1/A as much to produce as before. Equivalently, a fixed research budget
produces A times as much research output. This gain reflects a change in how
researchers work. They shift effort toward the steps AI does not make cheaper,
which become the bottlenecks. The ratio 1/A therefore compares the cost of the
best mix of steps with AI to the best mix without it, where both mixes yield the
same research output under (1).

What compute can do

Equation (11) shows three ways AI can raise the cost advantage. It can cut the
researcher’s remaining time 1 − σj . It can cut the cost of its own running time
(μ/w)τj . Or it can raise the quality qj of the output. Each type of improvement
has a different limit for how AI can accelerate research.

Faster or cheaper compute. Cheaper or faster compute lowers (μ/w)τj . Its gain is
limited, however, while researchers still have work to do on the step. Specifically,
recall that cj = qj / [(1 − σj) + (μ/w)τj]. As compute cost goes to zero, cj → qj/(1 − σj), a
ceiling set by the share of researcher time 1 − σj that remains. That is, even with
infinitely cheap compute, the cost advantage of AI may be limited, because the
parts of a step that still rely on human researchers (e.g., physical elements or
verification elements) remain expensive. Relatedly, compute matters more
when researchers have little left to do. As σj → 1 and with finite AI costs,
cj → qj · w / (μ · τj). Then halving the price or running time of compute doubles cj .
Cheaper compute has the biggest effect for work AI does alone.

Quality. Quality raises cj in proportion, with no ceiling. If qj > 1, then AI-assisted
output is simply better than what researchers produced at the same step,
possibly through better ideas, wider search, or fewer errors. If researchers
could not produce that output at any feasible cost, qj is effectively infinite and
so is cj . This is the channel through which AI can change what research
produces, in addition to what it costs.

Empirical implementation

Researcher time freed. We compute time freed for each step. Each step’s
share of researcher time freed is σj . The methodology for calculating time
freed at the step level, and its aggregation to subfields and fields, is described
earlier in this appendix.

AI running time and prices. No data source measures AI running time by step,
so we make an assumption that τj = 0.2 · σj , meaning that it takes 0.2 AI-hours
for each researcher-hour saved. A researcher-hour is assumed to cost w = $95,
which is taken as the median salary in the 2023 Survey of Doctorate Recipients
($155,000 in 2026 dollars) plus 28% benefits, over 2,080 hours. For AI, we
assume one agent actively working for an hour costs μ = $30, an illustrative
value based on public API list prices for Claude Opus 5.5. It is not a measured
average cost of Claude use or of Anthropic’s costs. We therefore have μ/w = 0.32,
and AI’s running time costs are (μ/w) · τj = 0.063 · σj . In other words, AI costs
about one-third as much per hour as a researcher, and it runs for only a fifth of
the researcher time it saves.

Other calibration parameters. We set θ = −1, so b = ½, which is the approximate
median ideas production function parameter across fields from Ahmadpoor
and Jones (2019). We also set qj = 1, so work done with AI is of the same quality
as researchers’ work alone.

Subfield acceleration. We combine these calibration assumptions with
Proposition 1 to compute the acceleration for each subfield. With these values,
cj = 1 / (1 − 0.937 · σj) ≥ 1. Notably, AI lowers the cost of every step where it frees
researcher time. Equation (12) then gives p̂ j = (1 − σj) + 0.063 · σj . That is, a step
keeps a share 1 − σj of its researcher time and adds AI time that costs 0.063 · σj of
its original researcher cost. The acceleration (10) becomes

\[
A_s=\left[\sum_j\omega_j(1-0.937\sigma_j)^{1/2}\right]^{-2} \tag{13}
\]

Field acceleration. A field’s acceleration is the average of its subfields’
accelerations, weighted by SciNet’s researcher counts for each subfield.

Scenarios. We compute the AI share and the research acceleration under three
scenarios. Scenario 1 reflects current AI capabilities, using the time freed σj
estimated from our survey and AI capability scores. In Scenario 1, every step
has σj equal to 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, or 1, depending on its AI capability score and the
levels of AI use reported in the survey. Scenarios 2 and 3 are hypothetical
futures in which AI fully automates 75% or 100% of non-physical (cognitive
and interpersonal) research steps, counted equally and starting with the steps
with the highest AI capability scores. Automated steps have σj = 1 and τj = 0.2.
The added automation never applies to physical steps. Every other step,
physical steps included, keeps its Scenario 1 values of σj and τj = 0.2 · σj .

We hold AI costs fixed across scenarios. In other words, AI extends what it can
do, but it does not become cheaper at its research tasks on average. To the
extent that AI inference costs fall per unit of time, or AI completes steps in less
time, the constant cost assumption is conservative. On the other hand, harder
tasks that AI does not yet perform, and more autonomous work on current
tasks, may require more tokens, more expensive models, or longer running
times, raising the cost of using AI. The overall AI cost trajectory in research will
be a balance of these forces.

F. Additional results

Comparison of economic primitives across scientific
and non-scientific usage

We compare sessions that the scientific work screener flags as scientific use with
sessions on the same product that it does not. This allows us to assess how
scientists’ use of Claude differs from that of non-scientists in terms of AI
autonomy, the time a task takes to complete for a human alone, the time AI can
save in a given task, the years of education needed to understand the user’s prompt, and the level of education somebody would need to understand Claude’s
response. For this purpose, we use classifiers introduced in Appel et al. (2026).

We find that scientists’ use is higher across virtually all of those dimensions,
both on Claude and Claude Code. Scientists’ usage provides AI with more
autonomy, especially on Claude Code. The task time-to-completion is often
more than twice as long for scientists’ applications, irrespective of Claude
product. Scientists use Claude for tasks where more time can be saved—with
the exception of administrative tasks—though that difference is stronger on
Claude than on Claude Code. Scientific use often requires four to seven more
years of education to conduct the underlying task or understand Claude’s
response associated with it.

Claude use by career stage

For users with Claude’s memory feature turned on, we use a classifier on Claude
sessions to identify their career stage and role from what their memory states.
As with our other classifiers, the memory-based classifiers in this study run in a
privacy-preserving analysis pipeline. Users can modify their memory or turn it
off entirely. Claude use among scientists differs by career stage. Doctoral
students use Claude most for data analysis (34.4%), whereas professors use it
most for writing and communication (44.0%). This is consistent with the
division of research tasks across career stages, as documented by
Robinson-Garcia et al. (2020), suggesting that AI usage tracks researcher needs.
The largest relative difference in use is in reading and knowledge acquisition,
where the share is about twice as high for doctoral students (8.8%) as it is for
professors (4.9%). This could imply that doctoral students spend more time on
this category of work, or that they have adopted AI more intensely for this
category of work than their faculty counterparts. Differences in the remaining
categories are small, at most 1.3 percentage points.
