All of a sudden you lose the flexibility of scale. How much does it cost me to manage, upgrade, connect, handle faults — all those things?

Guido Appenzeller: The agents will do all of that; that's no longer a problem.

Pat Gelsinger: Yeah, thank you. Everything gets easy.

Raghu Raghuram: So we've talked about compute, memory and networking. Power is the next one. You touched on it with vertical power delivery on the chip, but there's 800-volt DC, there's power delivery to the data center, there's an entire ecosystem all the way to power generation, and the sociopolitical dimensions of that. Where does innovation come in, and where is it pure execution?

Pat Gelsinger: If we start from first principles: our nation has done a terrible job with energy capacity. We went through 10 to 15 years where we were taking coal offline at the rate we were adding renewables, so the nation was essentially flatlined in overall energy capacity. Not good. Because in an AI digital age, energy capacity equals economic capacity. So essentially my economic capacity as a nation was flat for 15 years, if you buy that thesis, which I think is very provable. Over the last five years we've seen this enormous push to give me more capacity, but even with that we've gone to increasing our national energy capacity maybe 4% per year — from essentially zero to, wow, four percent. That's a bad situation. We just need more energy capacity, number one. Obviously we have some companies in that space; we've talked about nuclear uprating, with Alva. Nuclear baseload is a fabulous thing we want to build more of. Unfortunately, all our renewables have deep dependencies on China, which is very unfortunate for us, and gas turbine lead times are, what, only eight years at this point. So we have a difficult environment to scale up rapidly, but we just need more capacity, and fundamentally energy capacity dampens how fast we can go on AI. Why build a new data center and buy a million GPUs if I can't power them? I think you're going to see more and more defaults on many of those data center projects because the energy won't be there.

Raghu Raghuram: So you think it'll be a significant headwind?

Pat Gelsinger: I think it will be a headwind, and I think you're already starting to see some of the first indicators of that. The comment that just came out, I think, is the first of many you'll see. It's not that we'll slow down as a result, but people will start asking: do I really have the energy to underwrite the capital commitments I'm making — for cement, for data center racks, for GPU purchases? So it's going to be an upper bound. We need more energy capacity, we need more innovation in it, and we need more of those supply chains in the US. Nuclear is one of my favorites: when was the last nuclear reactor that came online in the US? It's 20 years ago. We just stopped a very capable industry. We also need to get much more efficient at delivering power through the networks, and, as you said, moving to 800-volt DC data centers, absolutely — being able to eliminate so many conversion steps that exist for historical and standardization reasons. We've got to fix this. With 800-volt DC, the revenge of Edison is upon us, I like to say. We need better power conversion. We have a couple of companies there; one of those, Vertical, I think will be a killer technology — being able to do 800 to 48, or 800 to 12, or even 800 to 5 in one conversion step, which gives you a lot more energy. Then there are solid-state transformers, switches, et cetera — rebuilding the power delivery network. I think there'll be a lot of innovation; power's cool again. And you'll need all of that in the cooling systems as well — turbines and refrigerants suddenly become exciting new technologies again. So for all of us hardware guys this is like a renaissance.

Raghu Raghuram: It's been 25 years since I had to go back and think about Carnot efficiency. Good stuff. How does that first law of thermodynamics work again?

Pat Gelsinger: Exactly. It's going to be an exciting time that way. But we also need to change the physics, because CMOS hasn't improved — the essential power per flop has been flat for the last five generations of NVIDIA chips. Not okay. That's where some of these things come in: we have a superconducting company, Snowcap, which affords the opportunity to fundamentally change the physics — a thousand times better power-performance. So I do think some of those innovations are upon us in the near future.

Pat Gelsinger: Hey — every innovation has always led to the next abstraction. What's new in our world today? Data structures, algorithms, abstractions — we bring it back to those foundations. I think the virtual machine abstraction, whether done at the infrastructure level or the application level, is still a foundational abstraction model that deserves to always have a place in the compute hierarchy.

Raghu Raghuram: So should one of us go back and run VMware?

Pat Gelsinger: That's what I told... it's hard to go back. And think about it: at VMware we essentially managed every aspect of the computing hierarchy — you managed the workload, the network, the storage, the system — and how do you abstract those? In the AI context, think about these agent swarms. Who's going to manage the agents? Who's going to create the security profiles around all the agents? Who's going to manage the performance of the agents? Essentially every fundamental element of virtualization and management needs to be recreated in this next computing hierarchy, and there are going to be lots of wonderful companies that break through in how those things get done. Do you want to end it on networking?

Guido Appenzeller: Actually, I'd love to stay with VMs for a second. Humans are a lot more patient than agents. So if you had to rebuild VMware today, how would it look different — VMware for agents?

Pat Gelsinger: Does there need to be a VMware for agents and one for humans? To me, yes, in the sense that I need humans setting the policies for the agents. I think it's an important point, because there needs to be — I'll use Dario's constitution — guardrails, the ability to set policies. Then you really say every aspect of a virtual machine as we know and love it today is servicing agents, as opposed to servicing hardware and humans. That becomes your fundamental design constraint on this side: how do I make agents great, how can I make them secure, how can I make them performant, how do I abstract them, how can I migrate them? All of those things will have new embodiments — vMotion for agents, just like every one of these will have a new embodiment for them. But I also need to think about how humans set the policies, describe the constitutions, get the dashboards, et cetera. That's where the interesting thing is: you need to do both, not just one. The fundamental hard design work we did for virtual machines was abstracting the hardware in a performant way for applications or operating systems. Here it's abstracting the hardware and operations for agents. It's a fundamental shift in whom you're serving.
