Vannevar Bush was a brilliant American engineer, born of a long line of Massachusetts boat men. In 1915, having already invented a novel surveyor’s tool, Bush enrolled as a grad student at MIT, and earned a PhD in electrical engineering in only nine months. He began teaching at MIT in 1919 and extended his track record of innovation. First, Bush developed a rectifier tube, which radically simplified home radio, and started a company called Raytheon to market his device. One of his proudest inventions was an analog computer for solving higher order differential equations. Bush was quoted saying that the computer represented an ideal: the ability to think straight in the midst of complexity. During World War II, the Defense Department would run Bush’s computer 24 hours a day.

Bush would go on to lead a long and esteemed life, advocating federal support of scientific research and technological advancement, ultimately leading to the creation of the National Science Foundation. Bush’s ascent from academia began with the immense opportunity that World War II provided.
